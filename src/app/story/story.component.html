A day spent exploring where offline technology could matter led through classrooms, shelters, events, and passenger accessibility to one smaller, testable idea.

01NOW
01THE SEARCH

Offline is not a feature.
It is a situation.

I started with a broad question: where could a nearby group of phones keep working when the internet could not? I explored classrooms, shelters, events, guided tours, and passenger accessibility.

The scenarios kept changing, but one thread held: accessibility is not only about translation. It is about making an important message readable, replayable, audible, and usable for different people at the same time.

02THE CABIN

The cabin made the problem tangible.

Most orders are fine.
That is not the whole story.

When a meal is not booked before a flight, crew take the order and enter it through an mPOS device. Most of the time, the system works. Under pressure, though, an order can be entered incorrectly or not entered at all. Passengers and crew can later remember the same exchange differently.

“The question was not whether the system could take an order. It was whether everyone had the same record of what happened.”

Language was more nuanced than I expected. In India, regional crew often bridge the gap. Abroad, English or another passenger may help. Emergency instructions already belong to formal crew procedures. The opportunity was moving somewhere else.

CONVERSATION, NOT ASSUMPTION

The crew conversation moved the research away from accusation and toward evidence: how could a passenger know that an important message was received, understood, and still available to replay?

03THE REFRAME

The research found a crowded market

Ordering is solved.
Communication is not.

Immfly, Omnevo, AirFi and other aviation platforms already cover passenger ordering, crew POS, inventory, offline payments and reconciliation. Building another meal marketplace would mean entering a mature, integration-heavy industry.

Then Boeing’s speech-to-text cabin concept offered a useful signal: the communication layer itself is still being explored. Not translation for its own sake. Not another chat app. One trusted announcement, delivered to many people, rendered in the form each person can use.

The next move

Start where the journey
lasts long enough to matter.

Not an airline ordering platform. A small offline accessibility layer for a bus, ferry, train, excursion boat, or guided coach: one local hub, one-tap announcements, replay, and a measured test of whether passengers join and staff keep using it.

The research converged on a question worth testing: can one staff message reach a shared journey in many usable forms?
